**Folk horror** is horror where land, community, and old belief are the threat. An outsider enters a closed place, the place has rules, and the rules require him. The name does two jobs. *Folk* carries the people, the parish, the inherited custom, and the folklore that turns out to be operating instructions rather than superstition. *Horror* is doing less work than usual here, because the genre rarely produces a monster; the community is the monster, and it does not think of itself that way.

The structural distinction against the two companion entries is the whole of it. [Lovecraftian horror]({{< relref "lovecraftian-horror.md" >}}) is realization: the world was always this bleak, and the character has just found out. [Redacted Weird]({{< relref "redacted-weird.md" >}}) is unreliable knowing: the truth exists and is withheld, erased, or eaten. Folk horror is **persistence**; the thing was never interrupted, and it kept going while the cities forgot about it. The consequence lands in the endings. The terminal state is not death and not madness, it is **incorporation**: you do not escape and you are not destroyed, you become part of the rite, and the calendar turns again next year without you.

## Alternate names

*Wyrd*, or *rural weird*, is the literary register, in the Alan Garner lineage; use it for prose. *Pagan horror* is pulpier and narrower; it overweights the cult and underweights the land. *Hauntology* is adjacent rather than identical: Ghost Box Records, Scarfolk, lost-broadcast nostalgia. It shares the texture without requiring the rite, so it is useful for aesthetic-anchored requests but is not a substitute for the genre.

The term itself was used by Piers Haggard about his own work and popularized by Mark Gatiss in *A History of Horror*.[^gatiss2010] Unlike cosmicism, it was named from outside; no author coined it as a philosophy.

## What it is not

The *backwoods slasher* is a rural setting with a killer in it and no belief system; *Texas Chain Saw* is a family, not a faith. *Cult horror* fails on age and self-awareness: a cult is recruited, recent, and knows it is deviant, while a folk horror community is inherited, old, and thinks it is normal. *Costume paganism* is flower crowns and antlers over a conventional monster plot; ask whether they have coherent ethics or only robes. *Cosmic horror* takes anything where the power in the field is indifferent and unbounded; folk horror's power is local, bounded, and reciprocal, and it wants a specific thing from a specific person.

The test is subtraction. Remove the community; does it still work? Remove the land; does it still work? Yes to both means this is not the genre.

## The folk horror chain

The chain has four links, sequential and causal. It replaces the three-of-seven theme test used in [File 001]({{< relref "redacted-weird.md" >}}) and [File 002]({{< relref "lovecraftian-horror.md" >}}), because the links depend on each other and counting them separately would measure the wrong thing. Adam Scovell set out the chain in *Folk Horror: Hours Dreadful and Things Strange*.[^scovell2017] It is the closest thing the genre has to a formal instrument, and it is the reason this file is structured differently from its companions.

1. **Landscape.** Terrain isolates, and then it shapes what grows there, socially and morally, not just agriculturally. The moor, the island, the fen, the forest edge, the valley with one road. It is not a backdrop, it is an agent with a preference. Seen in Summerisle, the Yorkshire moor of *Starve Acre*, the Northumberland coast of *Ghost Wall*, and Cornwall Coombe.
2. **Isolation.** Physical first, then social, then moral. The road floods and the ferry runs twice a week, but the real isolation is jurisdictional: no outside authority counts here, and everyone local already knows that. Seen in Sergeant Howie's warrant meaning nothing, the excommunicated family of *The Witch*, and the Swedish commune of *Midsommar*.
3. **Skewed belief and morality.** The group develops its own ethics, and they are internally coherent. That coherence is the horror. They are not confused, they are not hypocrites, and they are not evil to themselves. Lord Summerisle can explain the whole system calmly, and the explanation is the worst part of the film. Seen in Summerisle's theology, the Hårga's ritual calendar, the Waste in *Harvest Home*, and the hunters of *Kill List*.
4. **The happening, or summoning.** The rite completes: sacrifice, harvest, birth, burning. Unlike links 1 through 3, this one is optional in staging but not in implication; it may be withheld, offscreen, or arrive as a final image with no explanation. *Kill List* and *Ghost Wall* both hold it back and both qualify. Seen in the wicker man itself, the temple fire, the bog body, and Thomasin signing the book.

The application rule is short. Links 1 through 3 are mandatory and link 4 may be implied. Fail any of the first three and the work is out regardless of its score. This single rule does more filtering work than the whole seven-theme apparatus in the companion files, because backwoods horror reliably fails link 3 and cult horror reliably fails link 1.

### Candidate themes

These are not a scoring rubric; the chain does the scoring. They are vocabulary for describing why a given work lands.

| Theme | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| Land as agent and as memory | Soil that keeps a record. Ground that will not grow. A field that is owed something. |
| Outsider versus closed community | Newcomer, returning native, city visitor, ethnographer, policeman. Someone whose arrival is the plot. |
| The returning rite | Calendar, cycle, anniversary. Seven years, midsummer, harvest. It happened before and it will happen after. |
| Pagan survival under a Christian veneer | The church built on the older site. The vicar who knows. Doctrine as the thinner of two layers. |
| Fertility, harvest, sacrifice | Body as crop. Reproduction as civic duty. The economy and the religion are the same thing. |
| Folklore as operating manual | The rules work. Breaking them has consequences. Superstition was field-tested. |
| The ending you cannot outrun | Frequently visible from act one, which converts suspense into dread. Knowing does not help. |

## Six axes

The axes are subflavors. Use them to aim a reference-anchored request. Most strong works straddle two; name which.

| Axis | What it is | Exemplars | Trigger words |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unholy Trinity / British rural | The founding cluster. Period setting, rural England, institutional Christianity losing. | Witchfinder General (1968); Blood on Satan's Claw (1971); The Wicker Man (1973) | British, 70s, pagan, island, village |
| British TV strand | Where the genre actually lived. Single plays and children's serials, broadcast once. | Robin Redbreast (1970); The Owl Service (1969); Penda's Fen (1974); Children of the Stones (1977); A Ghost Story for Christmas (1971–) | TV, BBC, Play for Today, M. R. James |
| American folk / hollow | Transplanted. Puritan settlement, corn belt, Appalachian hollow, reservation. | Harvest Home (1973); Children of the Corn (1977); The Witch (2015); Old Gods of Appalachia; The Only Good Indians (2020) | New England, Appalachia, corn, settlers |
| Global folk | Non-Anglo traditions with their own chains. Least mapped, most upside. | Kwaidan (1964); Noroi (2005); Hagazussa (2017); Tumbbad (2018); Impetigore (2019); You Won't Be Alone (2022) | Japanese, Indonesian, Balkan, Alpine |
| Modern revival | Post-2010. Self-aware, slower, and usually inverting who the ignorant party is. | Kill List (2011); A Field in England (2013); Apostle (2018); Midsommar (2019); Lamb (2021); In the Earth (2021); Men (2022) | elevated, A24, Wheatley, Aster |
| Games | Thinnest column. First-person or point-and-click; the walk across the land does link 1. | The Excavation of Hob's Barrow (2022); Mundaun (2021); Blair Witch (2019) | play, game, interactive |

Axes cross. *The Witch* is American folk plus Unholy-Trinity period technique. *Midsommar* is modern revival plus global folk. Name both when recommending, because it tells the reader what they are actually getting.

## The folk horror problem

This is parallel in function to the Lovecraft problem in [File 002]({{< relref "lovecraftian-horror.md" >}}), but it comes in two halves and neither is about a single author's biography.

### Half one: the history is invented

The pagan-survival premise is largely a construction. Frazer's comparative method in *The Golden Bough* is discredited as method while remaining foundational as influence.[^frazer1890] Margaret Murray's witch-cult hypothesis, an organized pre-Christian religion surviving underground into the early modern period, is rejected by historians, with Ronald Hutton the standard correction.[^murray1921][^hutton1999]

The old ways the genre runs on were substantially assembled in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, not preserved from antiquity. This does not weaken the fiction. *The Wicker Man* is not worse for resting on bad anthropology; it is arguably better, because Summerisle's religion was designed by a Victorian landowner inside the film's own plot. It does mean that an authentic ancient rite is a fictional device, so any work claiming documentary status for its folklore is wrong and should be flagged as such.

### Half two: the politics cut both ways

The genre runs on fear of rural people, and that machinery can be driven in either direction. Both readings are live in the canon, often inside the same film.

| Reading | What it does | Where it shows |
|---|---|---|
| Reactionary | Blood, soil, continuity, and inheritance treated as virtues. Nativist material sits inside the machinery, the same way racial-purity anxiety sits inside cosmic horror's contamination theme. | The rooted community as the thing that survives; the rootless visitor as the thing that does not. |
| Condescending | Countryside as backward and murderous. The city visitor is the reasonable one, and his reasonableness is never examined. | Most of the British 1970s material, unexamined. |

The best modern work knows this and turns it. Stephen Graham Jones inverts whose land and whose rite is at stake.[^jones2020] *Midsommar* makes the American the ignorant party and gives the Hårga a fully coherent internal logic.[^aster2019] *Penda's Fen* rejects the reactionary reading outright from inside the tradition, which is the sharpest case in the genre.

The practical upshot for recommending is that the inverted material is where the live energy sits, the same way the reclamation axis carries File 002. Lead with it for anyone who found the classic British material politically hard to swallow. State the problem, do not sermonize.

## Search method

The procedure matches Files 001 and 002, with the theme count replaced by the chain and one genre-specific scoring rule added.

1. **Identify the axis.** A bare ask means spread across axes. A reference-anchored ask means narrow to that work's dominant axis. If the user names political discomfort with the classic British material, go straight to the inverted modern work.
2. **Generate candidates.** Vetted pool first, then the territory list. Cross-medium unless a medium is locked.
3. **Research live, always.** Never from memory. Query patterns: `<title> Goodreads rating`, `<title> Rotten Tomatoes critics audience`, `<title> Metacritic`, and `<title> folk horror retrospective`.
4. **Apply the floor.** Books 3.5/5. Film and TV 70% critics. Games 75 Metacritic. A user-set floor overrides.
5. **Walk the chain.** Links 1 through 3 are mandatory and link 4 may be implied. Fail any of the first three and the work is out whatever its score.
6. **Check the tally.** It includes the calibration set, which is excluded by default, and the cross-file exclusions.

### The audience-lag rule

This is the inverse of [File 002]({{< relref "lovecraftian-horror.md" >}})'s score-split rule, and it fires more often here. Folk horror is slow, unexplained, and ends badly on purpose, and audience aggregates punish exactly those three things, so critic and audience scores split hard and predictably.

| Work | Critics | Audience | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| The Witch (2015) | 91% | 61% | 30 points |
| Midsommar (2019) | 83% | 63% | 20 points |
| The Wicker Man (1973) | 91% | 82% | 9 points, does not fit the pattern |

The rule as it stands: the critic score carries, report both numbers when they split by more than fifteen points, and never drop a title on audience score alone. A low audience score is not a warning here; read it as confirmation that the film did its job.

One stated limitation. The rule rests on two clean data points, and the genre's own founding text does not fit it. The pattern appears to be specific to the slow modern revival rather than to folk horror at large, so treat it as provisional until four or five more splits are collected. It runs the other way too: a title with a high audience score and mediocre critics is usually a backwoods thriller wearing the iconography, so walk the chain before trusting it.

### Failure modes and false positives

- **Rural setting mistaken for the genre.** Test link 3. A killer in a field has no theology.
- **Cult mistaken for community.** A cult knows it is deviant; a community thinks it is normal. Test link 1 for how long they have been there.
- **Costume paganism.** Robes and antlers over a conventional monster plot. Test link 3 again; coherent ethics, or dressing?
- **Cosmic drift.** Unbounded, indifferent power belongs in [File 002]({{< relref "lovecraftian-horror.md" >}}). Folk horror's power is reciprocal and wants something specific.
- **Padding.** Nothing clears the floor, so weak fits get added. Say the pool came up short instead.
- **Availability blindness.** Recommending from the TV strand without checking whether the thing can actually be watched. Half of it is scarce.

One deliberate exception to the usual silent drop: a prize-winning work that fails the floor gets reported with its number rather than cut. *The Loney* is the standing example.
