**Climate change science** is the body of physical evidence describing how greenhouse gas accumulation alters Earth's energy balance, and the modelling apparatus that projects the consequences. Its central quantitative result — that doubling atmospheric carbon dioxide warms the surface by roughly 3 °C — has been stable since 1979, and the discipline's foundational physics predates the American Civil War. What has changed over five decades is not the answer but the confidence interval around it, and the resolution at which regional and temporal detail can be resolved.

This entry covers the historical development of the field, the track record of its projections, observed and projected effects, and the specific question of reversibility. It does not cover public opinion or policy sentiment, which are treated separately.

## Historical development

The greenhouse effect was identified in stages across the nineteenth century. Joseph Fourier established in 1824 that the atmosphere retains heat, making Earth warmer than incident sunlight alone can explain. **Eunice Foote** demonstrated experimentally in 1856 that carbon dioxide and water vapour absorb heat, and proposed that a CO₂-richer atmosphere would be warmer — work that predates John Tyndall's more precise 1859 laboratory measurements by three years and is frequently omitted from popular accounts. Svante Arrhenius produced the first quantitative calculation of CO₂-driven surface warming in 1896.

The twentieth century contribution was showing that the effect would actually accumulate. Guy Callendar argued in 1938 that warming was already underway and linked to fossil fuel combustion. The standing objection was that the ocean would absorb any excess; Roger Revelle and Hans Suess dismantled it in 1957 by demonstrating that oceanic CO₂ uptake is far slower than assumed. Charles Keeling began continuous atmospheric measurement at Mauna Loa the following year, converting a theoretical concern into an observed trend. Concentration reached approximately 425.7 ppm in 2025, about 52% above pre-industrial levels and higher than at any point in at least 800,000 years.[^friedlingstein2026][^wmo2025]

Modelling matured in parallel. Syukuro Manabe and Richard Wetherald's 1967 radiative-convective model, which handled heat transport, convective adjustment, and water vapour feedback together, is the direct ancestor of contemporary Earth system models. In 1979 a US National Academy of Sciences panel chaired by Jule Charney assessed **equilibrium climate sensitivity** (ECS) — the eventual warming from a CO₂ doubling — at 1.5–4.5 °C with a best estimate near 3 °C.[^charney1979] The IPCC was established in 1988 and has since completed six assessment cycles.

Assessed confidence in attribution hardened monotonically across those cycles: *very likely* human-caused in AR4 (2007), *extremely likely* in AR5 (2013), and in AR6:

> "It is unequivocal that human influence has warmed the atmosphere, ocean and land."
> — IPCC (2021)

| Assessment | Year | Assessed attribution |
|---|---|---|
| AR4 | 2007 | Warming *very likely* human-caused |
| AR5 | 2013 | Warming *extremely likely* human-caused |
| AR6 | 2021 | Human influence *unequivocal* |

## Projection track record

The fair test of a predictive science is whether its published projections match subsequent observation. Hausfather and colleagues evaluated seventeen model projections published between 1970 and 2007 and found most to be skillful, with residual error attributable primarily to incorrect assumptions about future emissions rather than to defective physics.[^hausfather2020] Correcting for the mismatch between projected and realised forcings substantially improved agreement.

ECS remained pinned at Charney's 1.5–4.5 °C range for four decades. AR5 declined to give a best estimate at all. The range finally narrowed in 2020, when a multi-line assessment combining process understanding, the historical record, and palaeoclimate evidence found values below 2 K difficult to reconcile with any line of evidence and gave a 66% range of 2.6–3.9 °C.[^sherwood2020] AR6 adopted a best estimate of 3.0 °C with a likely range of 2.5–4.0 °C.[^ipcc2021] The practical effect was asymmetric: very mild outcomes became implausible while the high tail persisted.

| Assessment | Year | Likely range | Best estimate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Charney Report | 1979 | 1.5–4.5 °C | ~3 °C |
| IPCC AR5 | 2013 | 1.5–4.5 °C | None given |
| Sherwood et al. | 2020 | 2.6–3.9 °C (66%) | ~3.1 °C |
| IPCC AR6 | 2021 | 2.5–4.0 °C | 3.0 °C |

Emissions scenarios evolved through IS92, SRES, RCPs, and the current **Shared Socioeconomic Pathways** (SSPs). The highest pathway — RCP8.5, later SSP5-8.5 — was widely and incorrectly treated as business-as-usual for years. It has since been retired as the core high-emissions baseline for CMIP7 and replaced with a lower "High" scenario, on the grounds that renewable cost declines, emergent climate policy, and observed emissions trends have rendered it implausible.[^vanvuuren2026] Projections revised upward rather than downward chiefly concern sea level, where ice-sheet dynamics were previously underrepresented.

### Projected warming by pathway

AR6 gives best-estimate warming above 1850–1900 for five SSPs across three periods. Reading across a row follows one storyline; reading down a column shows how much the choice matters at that date. The near-term column barely varies, which is the substantive point — warming to roughly 2040 is already determined by past emissions.[^ipcc2021]

| Pathway | 2021–2040 | 2041–2060 | 2081–2100 |
|---|---|---|---|
| SSP1-1.9 (very low) | 1.5 °C | 1.6 °C | 1.4 °C |
| SSP1-2.6 (low) | 1.5 °C | 1.7 °C | 1.8 °C |
| SSP2-4.5 (intermediate) | 1.5 °C | 2.0 °C | 2.7 °C |
| SSP3-7.0 (high) | 1.5 °C | 2.1 °C | 3.6 °C |
| SSP5-8.5 (very high) | 1.6 °C | 2.4 °C | 4.4 °C |

Each figure carries an uncertainty range of roughly ±0.3 °C near-term, widening beyond ±1 °C by 2100.

## Observed change

Most of what a climate report described as near-term projection twenty years ago is now measurement. 2024 was the warmest year in the 175-year instrumental record at 1.55 ± 0.13 °C above 1850–1900 — the first calendar year above the 1.5 °C mark.[^wmo2025] The human-attributable component was approximately 1.36 °C.[^forster2025] A single year above 1.5 °C does not constitute breach of the Paris limit, which is defined as a multi-decadal average, but it indicates the trajectory of that average.

The cryosphere and ocean provide the least ambiguous signals. The eighteen lowest Arctic sea-ice extents on record all fall within the past eighteen years, and each of the past eight years has set a new ocean heat content record.[^wmo2025] The fourth global coral bleaching event ran from January 2023, with bleaching-level heat stress affecting 84% of the world's reefs across 82 countries and territories by March 2025 — against 21% in 1998, 37% in 2010, and 68% across 2014–2017.[^icri2025] NOAA subsequently assessed the event as having likely concluded in mid-2025.[^noaa2025] Surface ocean acidification proceeds independently of temperature as a direct consequence of CO₂ uptake.

| System | Observed change | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Surface temperature | 2024 at 1.55 ± 0.13 °C; warmest of 175 years on record | Ongoing |
| Heat and extremes | More frequent and intense heatwaves; heavier precipitation; deeper drought; worse fire weather | Ongoing |
| Arctic sea ice | Eighteen lowest extents on record all within the past eighteen years | Ongoing |
| Ocean heat content | Eight consecutive record years | Ongoing |
| Coral reefs | Fourth global bleaching event; heat stress across 84% of reefs in 82 countries and territories | Likely ended mid-2025 |
| Ocean chemistry | Surface acidification proceeding with CO₂ uptake, independent of temperature | Ongoing |

## Committed change and reversibility

The long-term picture separates into processes that continue regardless of future emissions and processes that remain scenario-dependent. Sea level rise falls firmly in the first category, continuing for centuries under any pathway: AR6 projects 0.3–3.1 m by 2300 under SSP1-2.6 and 1.7–6.8 m under SSP5-8.5.[^ipcc2023] By 2100 the likely ranges are 0.28–0.55 m (SSP1-1.9) and 0.63–1.01 m (SSP5-8.5), with AR6 adding an explicit low-likelihood storyline in which a rise approaching 2 m by 2100 cannot be ruled out given deep uncertainty in ice-sheet processes.[^ipcc2021]

The **Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation** (AMOC) is very likely to weaken this century. AR6 assesses abrupt collapse before 2100 as unlikely with medium confidence, though several subsequent studies argue this understates the risk. This is a live disagreement rather than a settled question, and confident single figures in this area exceed what the evidence supports.

The most consequential finding for policy concerns what happens after emissions stop. Peak warming is governed by cumulative CO₂ emissions through the **transient climate response to cumulative emissions** (TCRE), which is approximately linear.[^matthews2008] The **zero emissions commitment** — additional warming realised after emissions cease — has a multi-model mean of −0.07 °C over fifty years, effectively zero.[^macdougall2020] The planet therefore does not carry decades of concealed warming that will arrive irrespective of action; surface temperature stabilises within roughly a decade of net zero.

This distinction matters because it separates the irreversible from the haltable. Ice-sheet loss, sea level rise, deep-ocean heat uptake, and extinction are irreversible on human timescales. Surface warming is not.

| Process | Timescale | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| Sea level rise | Centuries to millennia | Continues regardless of emissions; 0.3–3.1 m by 2300 (SSP1-2.6), 1.7–6.8 m (SSP5-8.5) |
| Ice sheets | Multi-century | Greenland and West Antarctic sectors at or near irreversibility thresholds |
| Permafrost | Decades to centuries | Thaw releases CO₂ and methane; amplifying feedback |
| AMOC | This century | Weakening very likely; abrupt collapse before 2100 unlikely (medium confidence), contested |
| Surface warming | Halts near net zero | Zero emissions commitment −0.07 °C over fifty years; effectively zero |

## Tipping elements

There is no single global threshold beyond which the climate system fails. The framework instead identifies discrete **tipping elements**, each with its own temperature threshold, characteristic timescale, and degree of reversibility.[^lenton2008] A 2022 reassessment found that exceeding 1.5 °C could trigger multiple tipping points and that several elements are at risk at warming already realised.[^armstrongmckay2022]

| Element | Approximate threshold | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| Warm-water coral reefs | ~1.5 °C, possibly 1.2 °C | Largely committed |
| Greenland ice sheet | ~1.5 °C for long-term loss | At or near threshold |
| West Antarctic ice sheet | ~1.5 °C for marine sectors | Some sectors may be committed |
| Permafrost | Gradual, no single threshold | Thaw underway |
| AMOC | ~4 °C central, wide uncertainty | Weakening likely; collapse not excluded |

Threshold estimates carry wide uncertainty and rest on limited palaeoclimate and modelling evidence; they function as risk indicators rather than deadlines. Coral is the clearest committed loss: IPCC SR1.5 projects a further 70–90% decline at 1.5 °C and near-total loss at 2 °C.[^ipcc2018]

## Remaining budget and mitigation capacity

A **carbon budget** is a cumulative total rather than a rate, which is why delay is more costly than it intuitively appears — each year of continued emission permanently removes options rather than merely postponing them. Approximately 170 GtCO₂ remained for a 50% chance of 1.5 °C from the start of 2025, roughly four years at current rates; an independent assessment puts the figure at 130 GtCO₂.[^friedlingstein2026][^forster2025] Fossil CO₂ emissions set a record of about 38 GtCO₂ in 2025.

Assessed warming under current policy has been revised downward, though less meaningfully than headline figures suggest. UNEP's 2025 assessment projects up to 2.8 °C on current policies and 2.3–2.5 °C on full implementation of national pledges, superseding the previous year's 3.1 °C and 2.6–2.8 °C.[^unep2025] Roughly 0.1 °C of that improvement derives from methodological updates rather than policy change, and UNEP notes that US withdrawal from the Paris Agreement will cancel approximately another 0.1 °C. The Climate Action Tracker's parallel assessment gives 2.6 °C on current policies, 2.2 °C on pledges and targets — a *worsening* from 2.1 °C, caused by that withdrawal invalidating US targets — and 1.9 °C if all net-zero targets are met.[^cat2025] The outlook has barely shifted across four consecutive annual updates.

**Carbon dioxide removal** (CDR) is assessed as unavoidable for reaching net zero, since some emissions cannot be eliminated.[^ipcc2022] Current removal runs at approximately 2 GtCO₂ per year, almost entirely through conventional forestry and land management; novel methods such as direct air capture and bioenergy with carbon capture contribute about 0.0013 GtCO₂ per year against a requirement of 7–9 GtCO₂ per year by 2050.[^smith2024] The scale-up implied is several orders of magnitude. Solar radiation management remains outside deployment consideration in the assessed literature: it addresses temperature without addressing ocean acidification, and carries termination-shock and governance risks.

| Limit | Remaining budget from start of 2025 | At current emissions |
|---|---|---|
| 1.5 °C | ~170 GtCO₂ (50% chance); 130 GtCO₂ on an independent assessment | ~4 years |
| 1.7 °C | Substantially larger | ~12 years |
| 2.0 °C | Larger still | ~25 years |

The three available levers differ sharply in maturity and in what they actually address.

| Lever | Status | Addresses the cause | Principal constraint |
|---|---|---|---|
| Emissions reduction | Proven, under-deployed | Yes | Rate of deployment, not technology |
| Carbon dioxide removal | Necessary, far from scale | Partially | Three orders of magnitude scale-up for novel methods |
| Solar radiation management | Unproven, not deployment-ready | No | Ocean acidification untouched; termination and governance risk |
