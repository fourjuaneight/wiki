**Climate change public opinion** is the measurement of what populations, governments, and institutions believe and do about climate change, a subject empirically distinct from the physical science and governed by different methods and failure modes. The headline finding is that large global majorities accept the science and want stronger action, and have for years. The analytically interesting questions lie elsewhere: in the gap between belief and priority, in the unusually wide partisan split within a small number of wealthy democracies, and in the systematic divergence between measured opinion and the opinion visible online.

This entry covers survey evidence on public attitudes, the gap between governmental rhetoric and policy, corporate positioning across its contradictory channels, demographic variation, and the distortion of apparent sentiment by platform dynamics. Method notes are given inline because in this field the method frequently determines the number.

## Global attitudes

The largest standalone survey on the subject is the UNDP **Peoples' Climate Vote**, whose 2024 edition reached more than 73,000 respondents speaking 87 languages across 77 countries, together roughly 87% of world population, using randomised mobile telephone calling conducted with the University of Oxford and GeoPoll.[^undp2024] Its 2021 predecessor used a different instrument entirely, recruiting through mobile-gaming advertisements across 50 countries; figures from the two editions are not directly comparable.

That survey found 80% wanting their government to strengthen climate commitments and 86% wanting countries to cooperate across geopolitical differences. Majority support held in large emitting nations, including 66% in both the United States and Russia and 67% in Germany. Fifty-six percent reported thinking about climate change daily or weekly and 53% reported greater worry than a year earlier.

| Peoples' Climate Vote 2024 finding | Share |
|---|---|
| Want their government to strengthen climate commitments | 80% |
| Want cooperation across geopolitical differences | 86% |
| Think about climate change daily or weekly | 56% |
| More worried than a year earlier | 53% |
| Say extreme weather is worse than last year | 43% |
| Never think about it | 11% |

A separate 25-nation study found a median of 67% describing climate change as a major threat, with 24% calling it minor and 9% no threat at all.[^pew2025a] Only Israel, Nigeria, and the United States lacked a majority calling it a major threat. Roughly 80% did so in Argentina, Brazil, France, Japan, and South Korea. Cutting against the global picture, the major-threat share has fallen significantly since 2022 in several wealthy countries including Greece, Italy, and the Netherlands. Rising global concern and falling rich-world concern are simultaneously true of different populations, and averaging them describes nothing.

### Belief versus priority

Conflating these two is the most common error in reporting on this subject. Asked to rank present worries across 31 countries, respondents place climate around eleventh, behind inflation, crime, and unemployment.[^ipsos2024] Asked instead which risk will dominate the next five to ten years, the same broad population ranks it first.[^axa2026] Both results are sincere; the apparent contradiction is a time-horizon effect. A population can hold a belief firmly and still subordinate it to the electricity bill at the ballot box, which explains a good deal of otherwise puzzling political behaviour.

### The emotional dimension

A ten-country study of 10,000 respondents aged 16 to 25, fielded in 2021 across Australia, Brazil, Finland, France, India, Nigeria, the Philippines, Portugal, the United Kingdom, and the United States, remains the reference measurement for **climate anxiety**.[^hickman2021] Fifty-nine percent reported being very or extremely worried and 84% at least moderately worried; 75% agreed that the future is frightening and 45% said the feelings negatively affected daily functioning. Distress ran higher in the Global South countries surveyed than in the wealthier ones, a pattern that recurs throughout the literature.

| Measure | Share of respondents |
|---|---|
| Very or extremely worried | 59% |
| At least moderately worried | 84% |
| Agreed that "the future is frightening" | 75% |
| Said feelings negatively affect daily functioning | 45% |
| Reported sadness, anxiety, anger, powerlessness, helplessness, or guilt | Over 50% each |

## Segmentation and demographic variation

For the United States, the **Six Americas** framework tracks six stable audience segments and is more informative than a single approval figure because it reveals directional movement.[^yale2025] As of Fall 2025 the Alarmed segment stands at about 25% and the Dismissive at about 11%, with the Alarmed outnumbering the Dismissive by more than two to one. The Alarmed grew from 11% in 2015 to 25% in 2025, a fourteen-point gain and the largest movement of any segment; Alarmed and Concerned together rose from 42% to 52% over the same period.

| Segment | Share (Fall 2025) | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| Alarmed, convinced, worried, want action now | ~25% | Growing |
| Concerned, convinced, less urgent | ~27% | Steady |
| Cautious, undecided on urgency and cause | ~18% | Slight growth |
| Disengaged, little knowledge, no strong view | Under 10% | Steady |
| Doubtful, sceptical but not hostile | ~11% | Steady |
| Dismissive, actively reject the science | ~11% | Steady |

### The wealth–concern paradox

Concern tends to be highest in countries most exposed to climate impacts and least responsible for causing them, and lowest in several of the wealthiest and highest-emitting nations. The two largest emitters, China and the United States, both sit among the less concerned populations surveyed. Broadly, Latin America and Africa report the most worry, followed by Europe, then Asia-Pacific, then the Middle East. This inversion, least responsible and most alarmed, constitutes the central political difficulty of the subject.

### Political alignment

The partisan gap in the United States is several times wider than in any other surveyed country and continues to widen. In 2026, 44% of American adults reported worrying about climate a great deal, the third-highest reading in a series running since 1989; within that total, Republicans registered 6%, a record low, against roughly 69% for Democrats and 45% for independents.[^gallup2026] Overall concern near a record high and Republican concern at a record low are simultaneous and non-contradictory: the aggregate is driven by Democrats and independents.

| United States, 2026 | Democrats | Republicans |
|---|---|---|
| Worry about climate "a great deal" | ~69% | 6% (record low) |
| Say climate change is mostly human-caused | 83% | 28% |

Party affiliation now predicts belief better than education, income, or direct experience of extreme weather. On this issue climate functions as an identity marker rather than an empirical proposition, which has implications for communication strategy; evidence-based persuasion performs poorly against identity-protective cognition.

### Generation and religion

The youth-concern narrative is supported but routinely overstated. Acceptance of human causation runs from 68% among Millennials down to 47% among the Silent Generation, a gradient of roughly twenty points rather than a chasm.[^prri2023] The same gradient appears inside conservative parties, with younger Republicans accepting human causation at higher rates than older ones.

| Generation | Accept human causation |
|---|---|
| Millennials | 68% |
| Gen Z | 67% |
| Gen X | 58% |
| Boomers | 55% |
| Silent Generation | 47% |

On religion the useful finding is a negative one. American white evangelical Protestants register the lowest climate concern of any major religious group, with the share calling climate a crisis falling from 13% in 2014 to 8% in 2023 even as the religiously unaffiliated rose from 33% to 43%.[^prri2023] The driver appears to be political alignment rather than theology: religious leadership, whether Catholic teaching following *Laudato Si'*, Islamic declarations, or creation-care movements, has consistently run ahead of congregations. Thirty-five percent of Americans agree that recent natural disasters are evidence of the end of times, rising to 62% among white evangelicals.

## Governments: rhetoric against policy

Two independent trackers convert national pledges into projected warming and function as the field's scoreboard. UNEP's 2025 assessment projects up to 2.8 °C on current policies and 2.3–2.5 °C on full pledge implementation, superseding the prior year's 3.1 °C and 2.6–2.8 °C.[^unep2025] The Climate Action Tracker gives 2.6 °C on current policies, 2.2 °C on pledges and targets, and 1.9 °C if every net-zero target is met.[^cat2025]

| Scenario | UNEP (2025) | Climate Action Tracker (2025) |
|---|---|---|
| Current policies in force | Up to 2.8 °C | ~2.6 °C |
| All national pledges implemented | 2.3–2.5 °C | ~2.2 °C |
| Every net-zero target met | — | ~1.9 °C |

The apparent improvement is substantially illusory. UNEP attributes roughly 0.1 °C to methodological updates rather than policy change and notes that US withdrawal from the Paris Agreement will cancel approximately another 0.1 °C; the Climate Action Tracker attributes its own 0.1 °C drop largely to a revised treatment of China's emissions pathway. On the pledges measure the outlook actually *worsened*, from 2.1 °C to 2.2 °C, because that withdrawal invalidated US targets outright. The projection has barely moved across four consecutive annual updates, against roughly 3.6 °C estimated on existing policy in 2015.

Three patterns recur across wealthy democracies: climate policy softens on collision with household costs, agricultural regulation produces the sharpest backlash, and headline targets survive while implementing mechanisms are quietly loosened. In the developing world the framing is more consistent, invoking historical responsibility, the right to develop, and the demand that responsible parties fund consequences. That demand produced the Loss and Damage Fund, operationalised in 2023, against which the UN climate body records commitments of approximately $661 million; estimated annual need by 2030 exceeds $400 billion.[^unfccc2023] Reported totals vary between roughly $661 million and $725 million by source and cut-off date.

The COP28 outcome was the first to name fossil fuels explicitly:

> "Transitioning away from fossil fuels in energy systems … so as to achieve net zero by 2050."
> — UNFCCC Global Stocktake (2023)

## Corporations: three contradictory channels

Asking what a company thinks about climate change yields three different answers depending on which channel is queried, and the contradiction between them is the finding rather than a confound.

- **Public commitment** is widespread and growing, with thousands of firms holding externally validated emissions targets, but validation is not delivery, and the standard-setting body removes companies for failing to follow through.
- **Lobbying** is where money moves and where alignment is weakest. Independent tracking finds the share of engaged companies whose advocacy matches science-based guidance roughly tripled between 2020 and 2024, but from a very low base, leaving the large majority still misaligned. Trade associations frequently lobby against positions their member companies publicly endorse.
- **Insurance pricing** is the most credible signal because no audience is being performed for. Carriers have withdrawn from or sharply repriced high-risk US markets, and an industry whose entire business is pricing risk correctly has concluded the risk is rising.

| Channel | What it shows | Reliability as a signal |
|---|---|---|
| Public commitment | Widespread, growing, unevenly delivered | Low, cheap to issue, weakly enforced |
| Policy lobbying | Majority still misaligned with stated positions | High, money and effort are committed |
| Insurance pricing | Withdrawal and repricing in exposed markets | Highest, own capital at stake, no audience |

Documented internal knowledge complicates the denial narrative. ExxonMobil's internal projections from 1977 to 2003 forecast warming at 0.20 ± 0.04 °C per decade, with 63–83% of those projections meeting accuracy standards, while the company publicly cast doubt on the science.[^supran2023]

The reporting narrative since 2024 has been corporate retreat, and some of it is real. But analyses tracking behaviour rather than announcements find most firms holding steady or accelerating while going publicly quiet, a pattern termed **greenhushing**. Silence is therefore weak evidence of abandonment in either direction.

## Online discourse and false social reality

The most consequential finding in this literature concerns a near-universal misperception. A representative study of 6,119 Americans found that 80–90% underestimate how many of their fellow citizens support major climate policies: actual support runs 66–80% while perceived support is 37–43%.[^sparkman2022] Supporters outnumber opponents roughly two to one, and almost nobody knows it, including the supporters. The authors term this a **false social reality**.

The mechanism matters because people calibrate their willingness to speak, campaign, and vote against perceived social norms. A silent majority that believes itself a minority behaves like a minority. A 2026 follow-up complicates any simple remedy, finding misperception running in both directions, since populations underestimate widely-held attitudes while overestimating rare behaviours such as donating or protesting, and finding that correcting the misperception did not reliably change behaviour.[^tiede2026] The gap is robust; the fix is not.

Several factors distort apparent sentiment online. Automated accounts contribute materially to climate posting volume, concentrated on the denial side rather than the activist side, so volume is not a headcount of humans. Engagement-ranked feeds structurally promote conflict, since disagreement between two entrenched minorities generates more interaction than agreement between two large majorities. Platform cultures diverge sharply; Reddit's climate communities skew science-oriented, its collapse-focused communities are fatalist rather than denialist, and X hosts the sharpest denial, with discourse there shifting markedly after the 2022 change of ownership. Academic research over-samples Twitter because its data was historically easiest to obtain, which distorts what is known about every other platform.

Social media research on Latin America found YouTube exerting the strongest positive effect on climate awareness, ahead of Instagram and Twitter.[^gomezcasillas2023] The general conclusion is that online volume is a poor proxy for population sentiment and a worse proxy for behaviour.

## Methodological cautions

Survey evidence in this field carries recurring limitations that should condition any claim built on it. Instruments measure stated attitudes rather than behaviour, and the gap between the two is itself a major finding. Method changes the answer: telephone surveys, online panels, and in-game advertisements reach systematically different populations, making cross-survey comparison rough at best. Coverage is uneven, with Africa, Central Asia, and the Middle East chronically under-surveyed, so confident global statements typically rest on data from a few dozen wealthy and middle-income countries. Annual series revise, and quoting one year's projection alongside another's is a frequent source of error. Where reputable surveys disagree, reporting both figures is more honest than averaging incompatible methods.
